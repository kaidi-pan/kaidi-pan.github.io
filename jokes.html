[CW: slightly vulgar, no offense intended. On the Lubrication of Modal Furniture]

Possible worlds semantics is the WD-40 of philosophy. It’s cheap, versatile, and a household staple since roughly 1963. Whenever a piece of your furniture develops a modal squeak, you give it a spray.

But life has more intimate moments when something delicate needs lubricating. You are not sure if WD-40 can be sprayed directly there. So there's something fancier on the market: pricier, gentler, organic, hypoallergenic, and sensitive enough to tell apart necessarily equivalent things. That’s the hyperintensional machinery, the fancy stuff.

The analogy runs deeper, because the field has split just like the lubricant market:

The intensionalists say WD-40 is fine, even for that. You just haven’t sprayed it properly.

The impossible worlds theorists agree, sort of. They recommend WD-40 PLUS: the same trusted brand, now with impossible worlds added for sensitive skin.

The hyperintensional enthusiasts, having discovered the good stuff, decide it’s the real deal and start using it on the tables and chairs too. WD-40 is just a cheaper copy of the real stuff.

The structured propositionalists instead swear by an older formula. It comes with a DIY kit. It's so complicated, and whether the mixture is actually a lubricant or just ingredients standing next to one another is something they've been arguing since 1903. They say you can still spray it on places where WD-40 doesn't work.

The deflationists report that they’ve only ever needed lubricant for the furniture. You incorrectly assume you actually need lube for other things.

The modal skeptics will tell you nonchalantly that WD-40 is actually just a water repellent, so the squeak will come back after a while anyways.

And then there's the funniest reactions of all, from the old-school primitivists, shouting, “Goddammit, just use olive oil like the ancient Greeks!” Aristotle had essence and modal talk without any of this machinery. This usually comes out after a few drinks. The fancy organic hypoallergenic premium lube, they say, is just the good old olive oil, well before anyone invented WD-40.

[Interrogatives]

During the time when Oxford was still the front and center of Ordinary Language Philosophy, a philosopher would go around and argue that how-questions are better than why-questions. People ask him why, he said: "when you meet someone, you normally ask 'how are you', not 'why are you'."

(From Tim Williamson.)

[A very random fun fact about the English language]

People say English is confusing, but my favorite example is "and/or." In English we sometimes write "and/or" to make it clear that or is inclusive. If "and" means and, and "or" means or, then what does the slash do? It means… and/or.

So "and/or" is actually "and and/or or," which, if repeated enough times, will eventually summon a logician.

(From Simon Charlow during a lecture.)

[Presuppositions]

Notice the perhaps unintended irony of the ending of Strawson (1950) On Referring, a historical landmark on the literature on presuppositions:

"Neither Aristotelian nor Russellian rules give the exact logic of any expression of ordinary language; for ordinary language has no exact logic."

(This was drawn to my attention by Larry Horn during a lecture. I don't think Strawson himself realized the profound irony here.)

[Russell's recollection of Wittgenstein]

"He used to come to see me every evening at midnight, and pace up and down the room like a wild beast for three hours in agitated silence. Once I said to him: 'Are you thinking about logic, or about your sins?' 'Both', he replied, and continued his pacing. I did not like to suggest it was time for bed, for it seemed probable both to him and to me that on leaving me he would commit suicide."

Not sure about committing suicide, but serious readers of the Tractatus would understand that Wittgenstein was not joking at all when he responded "both" there.

[Semantics vs pragmatics, a cliché joke]

Professor: "Affirming the denial is denial, denying the denial is affirmation. Remember, double-negation cancels out!"

Student: "How about affirming the affirmation?"

Professor: "Well, it's still affirming. No language in the world will have double-affirmation as negation, that never happens."

Student: "Yeah right."

(Sources of this joke remain unclear. Some credit it to Sidney Morgenbesser in response to J. L. Austin, and some credit it to Ludwig Wittgenstein.)

[CW: sexual description. About teleology]

In Molière's satire, Le Malade Imaginaire (1673), "Aristotelian" views about the form-based causal powers or virtues of substances are famously ridiculed: "I am asked by the learned doctor for the cause and reason that opium makes one sleep. To this I reply that there is a dormitive virtue in it, whose nature it is to make the senses drowsy."

Molière's mockery wishes to show how explanatorily futile it is to appeal only to teleological explanations. But there's a vulgar joke that nicely articulates a counterpoint — explanations without specifying means-ends structures can be blind:

X and Y are trying to have sex for the first time. But X is a fool, he has no idea what to do. Y has to instruct him how to do it: "You see this hole here? Now, slide your thing in, and then pull it out a little, and then push it back in, then pull..."

X cuts Y off: "Wait wait wait hold on, push it in or pull it out? Make up your mind first!"

*variant 4* (rewritten from G. Strawson (2010) The Depth(s) of the Twentieth Century)

I want something because I try to get it. Because if I don't try to get it, I don't want it.

I believe something to be true because I am disposed to act on it. Because if I am not disposed to act on it, I don't believe it.

I know what words mean because I use them correctly. Because if I don't use them correctly, I don't know what words mean.

I am angry because I show anger. Because if I don't show anger, I am not angry.

I am conscious because I behave in certain ways. Because if I don't behave in certain ways, I am not conscious.

There's a table in front of me because I have certain table-ish experience. Because if I don't have certain table-ish experience, there isn't a table in front of me.

A is fragile because it would break if I dropped it. Because if it wouldn't break if I dropped it, A is not fragile.

A causes B because B always follows A. Because if B doesn't always follow A, A wouldn't cause B.

I exist because I have an effect on other things. Because if I don't have an effect on other things, I don't exist.

[Saying/showing in the Tractatus]

A: I wonder why you rich people can always succeed in extracting all the money from my pocket?

B: It's easy — but I can't divulge my secret for nothing. First, you must give me five bucks.

A: Alright (— proceed to give B the money)

B: First, you take a dead fish; you cut off its head and put its entrails in a glass of water.

A: And, if I do this, will I become rich?

B: No, not so quickly. If you wish me to continue, I'd need five more bucks.

A: Hmmm, ok (— proceed to give another five bucks)

B then continues in a similar vein. Demanding more and more money, and so on and so forth...

Soon afterward, A all of a sudden explodes in fury: "You rascal, I see what you are aiming at; there is no secret at the bottom of this at all!!!"

"That," replies B, as he returns the money, "is the secret"

(This joke is collected from Jim Conant's 1991 paper "The Search for Logically Alien Thought". To which, he commented: "Certain general features of the Tractatus's mode of elucidation are reflected in the following tale... [A] will be relieved of his perplexity about [B] — and the lesson will be complete — when he recognizes that the source of his attraction to [A] has nothing to do with [A] and everything to do with himself." A similar structure of elucidation applies to certain themes in Buddhist philosophy, Pyrrhonism, and so on.)

[Pragmatics]

A man is asking a farmer about his two cows.

Man: About how much milk do you get from them in a day?

Farmer: Which one, the brown one or the black one?

Man: The brown one.

Farmer: About half a gallon

Man: And the black one?

Farmer: About half a gallon.

Man: Ok... what do you feed them?

Farmer: Which one?

Man: Brown.

Farmer: She eats grass.

Man: How about the black one?

Farmer: She eats grass too.

Man (frustrated): Why do you keep asking me which one when the answers are the same?

Farmer: Because the brown cow is mine!

Man: Oh, and the black one?

Farmer: It's mine too.
